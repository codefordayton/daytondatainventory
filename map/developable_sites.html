Methodology & sources

How this layer was built, what it includes, and where it should not be trusted.

This is a screening tool, not a vetted list. Nothing here has been checked for easements, deed restrictions, environmental condition, utility access or zoning suitability. A parcel appearing here means the County classifies it as vacant land, or the City’s condition survey recorded a non-residential building as vacant. It does not mean anything can be built on it.

The question this answers

The 2026 Bowen study recommends inventorying “undeveloped land and underutilized structures (e.g. vacant schools, churches, warehouses) that could support residential development or adaptive reuse.” This is a first pass at that inventory, aimed at a developer asking where could something be built — not at a resident asking what is already being built. The City’s Housing Dashboard answers that second question and is a separate product.

What is included, and on what basis

TypeCountRule
Vacant land11,816 County land use code 300 industrial, 400 commercial or 500 residential vacant land. Any owner.
Vacant structure91 A church, school or warehouse parcel carrying a building that the Housing Condition Survey records as vacant, boarded, or slated for demolition.

The structure rule matters: 595 parcels in those same land uses were excluded because the survey records them occupied. This is measured vacancy, not an assumption that every church or warehouse is available. 43 candidate parcels had no survey record at all and are excluded — a building the survey never visited will not appear here regardless of its actual state.

Size — and a trap worth knowing

The County’s own acreage field is unusable on its own. ACRES is blank or zero on 89% of Dayton parcels. Zero there means not recorded, not small — most city lots are measured by frontage and depth instead. A size distribution built on that field looks entirely plausible and is almost entirely artifact. An earlier version of this layer made exactly that mistake.

Acreage here is derived from parcel geometry — the mapped polygon area from DaytonParcels — converted with a factor calibrated against the 271 parcels that do carry a recorded acreage. Derived values match the recorded ones to three decimal places.

Both figures ship in the underlying data (acres, acres_geometry, acres_best) so the provenance of any number stays visible.

How sites were placed on the map

11,639 of 11,907 (97.8%) have coordinates, resolved in this order:

  • City address point, where the parcel has an addressed location
  • else parcel centroid from City DaytonParcels
  • else parcel centroid from Montgomery County mc_parcel_polygon

The remaining 268 are absent from both parcel layers. 229 of them carry a street-only address with no house number (GIBBS LN, W 3RD ST) — the signature of rights-of-way, remnant strips and rail corridors that appear on the tax roll without a mapped polygon. Address geocoding cannot place them either, since there is no house number to geocode. They are left unplaced rather than approximated: a marker in the wrong location is worse than no marker.

Owner categories are heuristic

The city / land bank / other public / company / individual grouping is a pattern match on the owner-name string. It is used only to categorise owners, never to decide who owns a parcel.

The distinction matters because owner-name matching is genuinely error-prone here. A naive search for owners containing “city” and “Dayton” across the tax roll returns 2,904 parcels, of which 123 belong to other entities — 107 of them the Dayton City School District, a separate legal body. Tightening the match naively fails too, because the roll writes the City five different ways (CITY OF DAYTON, THE CITY OF DAYTON, DAYTON OH CITY OF, and others). The categoriser here is curated for those cases, but the labels remain worth spot-checking.

Assembly potential

Each site carries how many sites its owner holds in this layer, and their combined acreage. Contiguous lots under a single owner are the practical route to a site larger than one parcel, and they do not look like sites when read parcel by parcel.

This is a proxy, not adjacency. It counts an owner’s holdings anywhere in the city, not just neighbouring lots. True adjacency would need a geometry pass.

Known limits

  • Not vetted. No check for easements, deed restrictions, environmental status, utility access or zoning.
  • Land use codes describe assessment, not potential. LUC 500 means the Auditor classes a parcel as residential vacant land. It says nothing about buildability.
  • Structure vacancy depends on survey coverage. A building the survey did not visit will not appear.
  • 268 sites are unplaced, mostly rights-of-way and remnant strips.
  • Approved plans and proposed projects are not included. No public dataset links approved plans to parcels; compiling them remains manual work — and is precisely the gap that prompted this.

Reproducing it

Every step is scripted and re-runnable from public sources. No licensed data, no restricted access, no manual editing of the outputs. The pipeline, field-level data dictionaries and the full set of caveats live in the project repository.
